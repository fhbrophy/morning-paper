Worth a look
A 1,000-mile cloud on Mars, explained

Every Martian spring morning, a thin cloud forms near the Arsia Mons volcano and stretches over 1,000 miles. A new Nature Geoscience model says it forms by homogeneous nucleation, where water vapor turns straight into ice without dust to grow on. That process has been in textbooks but has never been seen in nature, and it needs a 30°C drop in 10 minutes.

Why it's interesting: It suggests Mars's air gets far more humid than anyone thought.

Source: Ars Technica

An ice age started 2,000 years ago, until we canceled it

Dead moss under Baffin Island's Barnes Ice Cap, the last piece of the Laurentide Ice Sheet, shows the ice started growing again about 2,000 years ago. A study in Science reads that as the start of the next glaciation, which kept going until greenhouse warming reversed it around 1960. Today none of the ice cap is above the snow line, so it is melting away.

Why it's interesting: It's direct evidence that people interrupted one of Earth's most regular cycles.

Source: Ars Technica

One positron, trapped for months

Northwestern physicists trapped a single positron in a supercooled Penning trap and can hold it for months. With it they can measure the particle's magnetism about 30 times more precisely than before, and compare it with the electron's.

Why it's interesting: Any difference between matter and antimatter here would point to physics beyond the Standard Model.

Source: Phys.org

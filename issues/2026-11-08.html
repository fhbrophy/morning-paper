Data centers: about 2,000 marched in Dublin yesterday

Ireland’s first national protest against new data centers drew about 2,000 people in Dublin on Saturday. Marchers walked from Parnell Square to Leinster House and called for a moratorium, citing carbon emissions, water and noise, and electricity prices. Organizers said Ireland already has more than 100 data centers and that they do not create many jobs. A government-commissioned report has claimed €22 billion added to the economy since 2010, about 17,000 jobs, and €2.8 billion in tax; Enterprise Minister Peter Burke called further expansion a “strategic opportunity.”

Why it matters: You’ll be in Dublin next weekend, and the same power-and-community fight is shaping campus jobs in Arizona and the Carolinas too.

Source: RTÉ

Worth a look
Hit the wrong side of an asteroid and the nuke might not work

Sandia National Laboratories physicists found that an asteroid’s lumpy shape can change how well a nuclear X-ray blast deflects it by nearly fivefold: 3.9 millimeters per second from one direction versus 0.8 from another on the same object, and spin-up varying by as much as a factor of 17. A new simulation that is more than 1,600 times faster than older models lets them use real spacecraft-mapped shapes instead of pretending asteroids are spheres.

Why it’s interesting: Planetary defense may come down to aiming at the right ridge, not just packing a big enough punch.

Source: ScienceDaily / Sandia

MIT reprograms immune cells inside the body

MIT engineers used antibody-tagged lipid nanoparticles to deliver mRNA that turns a patient’s own natural killer cells into CAR-NK cells, skipping the lab step of extracting, editing, and reinfusing them. In mice they modified about 80% of the targeted NK cells; the treatment wiped out T-cell lymphoma after five injections and reversed autoimmune hepatitis damage after one. The work is in Nature Biotechnology.

Why it’s interesting: If it works in people, a nanoparticle infusion could replace a weeks-long, hugely expensive cell-manufacturing pipeline.

Source: ScienceDaily / MIT

An atom-scale model for growing better silicon carbide

Penn State researchers refined a reactive force-field model of how silicon carbide turns from solid to vapor inside a crystal-growth furnace—the process used to make wide-bandgap wafers for high-power electronics. Because the furnace is too hot and closed to watch directly, the simulation tracks which silicon- and carbon-rich molecules leave as the temperature rises, a mix that can cause droplets, defects, or the wrong crystal structure if it is off.

Why it’s interesting: Getting the vapor recipe right in software could cut the trial-and-error that makes SiC wafers expensive.

Source: Phys.org / Penn State

Software wrings more planets out of Webb’s images

Astronomers built FLIP, a physics-based model of Webb’s NIRCam with about a million tunable parameters, then used gradient descent on a GPU (about half an hour) to match the telescope’s real point-spread function. On known exoplanet hosts it boosted signal-to-noise by 1.5 to 2 times and revealed a planet in a carbon-dioxide wavelength for the first time in the Webb data; on a noisy comparison star, sensitivity rose by a factor of 15.

Why it’s interesting: The hardware is already in space; better software is how you find the dimmer, older planets it was just missing.

Source: Ars Technica

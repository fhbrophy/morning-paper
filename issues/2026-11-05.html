Job market: private hiring beat expectations

ADP says private employers added 86,000 jobs in October, ahead of forecasts of 69,000 to 75,000. Trade, transportation, and utilities led with 39,000, and construction added 13,000, while professional and business services lost jobs for a third straight month. Job changers got 4.8% raises versus 3.0% for people who stayed put. Friday's official report is expected to show about 65,000 jobs and 4.2% unemployment.

Why it matters: Switching jobs is still paying better than staying, which helps your case in any offer talk.

Source: ADP · CNBC · Reuters via Investing.com

Data centers: voters said no almost everywhere

CNBC and Ballotpedia counted 41 data center ballot measures in 10 states, and voters sided against data centers in every one except Harwood, North Dakota. Ohio had 21 of them. Meanwhile, building keeps going: Lambda announced a $33.1 billion campus in Lowndes County, Alabama, with construction starting early 2027 and more than 225 permanent jobs, and Fluor signed the construction contract for three TeraWulf buildings in Kentucky. OpenAI also launched a national program to let utilities curtail its campuses' power during grid stress.

Why it matters: Developers are now promising to pay their own grid costs and cut load on demand to win approval, and new campuses still mean ops jobs.

Source: CNBC · Lambda · Fluor · TechTarget

Worth a look
Heisenberg's reactor was never close

Researchers studied two surviving uranium cubes from Germany's WWII B8 reactor and the archives behind it. Heisenberg claimed a bit more uranium would have done it, but they estimate the reactor needed 1,292 to 1,775 cubes instead of 664, plus twice the heavy water Norway's only plant made in the whole war. Impure German graphite would have doomed a graphite design too.

Why it's interesting: It settles an 80-year debate about how close the Nazi bomb program came.

Source: Ars Technica

A telescope digital twin finds a hidden planet

Northwestern astronomers built a physics model of the James Webb telescope that accounts for nanometer-scale wobbles and mirror shifts, then used it to subtract a star's glare. Run on two-year-old data, it improved sensitivity 2 to 15 times and revealed the giant planet 51 Eridani b where standard processing saw nothing.

Why it's interesting: Better modeling of an existing machine beat building a more stable one.

Source: Phys.org · Nature

Why the Grand Canyon is missing a billion years

The Great Unconformity is where 540-million-year-old rock sits right on top of rock over a billion years old, with everything in between gone. New mantle-flow models in Nature suggest slow currents deep in the Earth lifted whole continents for hundreds of millions of years, eroding the record, then let them sink so seas buried the worn surface.

Why it's interesting: One of the best places to see it is a few hours north of Mesa.

Source: Phys.org · Nature

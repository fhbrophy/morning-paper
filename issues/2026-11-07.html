The news
Jobs: +165,000 in October, far above the 59k call

BLS reported Friday that nonfarm payrolls rose 165,000 in October versus a Dow Jones forecast of about 59,000. Unemployment edged down to 4.1%. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4% on the month and 3.1% year over year. Prior months were revised up (August to 186,000, September to 60,000). Gains were broad: professional and business services +70,000, healthcare +42,000, transportation and warehousing +25,000, manufacturing +19,000. Long-term unemployment still climbed, with average duration at 27.1 weeks.

Why it matters: A hot print raises December Fed hike odds and undercuts soft-landing cut hopes that had been supporting growth stocks.

Source: CNBC

AI / data centers: OpenAI drops NDAs with public officials

After midterm backlash against data-center expansion, OpenAI said it will not use NDAs with public officials on community projects, limiting secrecy to narrow security and trade-secret cases. It also pledged to pay the full cost of the power and infrastructure it uses, publish expected local jobs, and put a “local compact” on every future compute campus it owns. Amazon made a similar NDA shift with agencies in October.

Why it matters: Politics is shaping how campuses get built, which shapes the ops and controls jobs that follow.

Source: The Verge

Worth a look
A robot built a truss in orbit—without astronauts

Caltech’s ASTRA truss builder, part of DARPA’s NOM4D program, autonomously assembled a bay of a 1.4-meter reflector antenna in low Earth orbit—the first time a structure has been built in space without crew. The payload hitchhiked on Momentus’s Vigoride 7 after a March 30 launch; time limits mean the full 11-bay truss may not finish before the mission ends in December, but the concept worked.

Why it’s interesting: Once structures are too big for fairings, the only path left is building them up there.

Source: Phys.org / Caltech

A fingernail chip untangles mixed laser light

Stanford engineers and collaborators showed a photonic chip that takes partially coherent light—mixed lasers whose photons fall out of step—and sorts it into clean, mutually incoherent beams. It’s the first experimental demo of an idea theorized in the 1980s, with possible uses in microscopy, astronomy, and sensing where overlapping sources usually blur together.

Why it’s interesting: Cameras throw away depth; separating coherence modes can keep more of what the light knows.

Source: Phys.org / Stanford · Nature Photonics

Flexible connectors flip nanoparticle assembly dogma

MIT researchers used superflexible, charged connector molecules so nanoparticle networks reorganize by themselves instead of needing rigid, pre-aimed bonds. The counterintuitive approach produced new crystal structures and could make custom materials for photonics, catalysis, and medicine easier to scale.

Why it’s interesting: Twenty years of “make the ligands rigid” just got a useful opposite extreme.

Source: Phys.org / MIT

AI that helps engineers see the whole earthquake picture

University of East London researchers sketched a human-supervised “proto-AGI” workflow that joins soil, ground-motion, and structural models instead of assessing them in silos. On a five-story concrete building hit with Northridge and Kobe records, Northridge produced about 3.4× the median seismic demand of Kobe, but peak structural movement differed by only 1.32×—a reminder that stronger shaking doesn’t scale one-to-one into damage.

Why it’s interesting: The pitch isn’t replacing judgment; it’s giving structural engineers one coherent picture before they decide.

Source: Phys.org / UEL

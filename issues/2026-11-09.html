Worth a look
Listening to electrical noise to spot paired electrons

Physicists in a Science study led by Milan Allan used the noise in electrical current to tell whether electrons in a superconductor have paired up, a way to probe a long-standing mystery without relying on indirect signatures.

Why it’s interesting: Noise measurements as a diagnostic could sharpen the hunt for better superconductors.

Source: Ars Technica

An implant that zaps excess sugar out of blood

A wirelessly powered implant uses gold flakes dotted with platinum to drive an electrochemical reaction that converts glucose into a byproduct the kidneys flush out in urine. It has been tested in mice.

Why it’s interesting: An electrical device doing the work of insulin-based treatment is a neat bit of power electronics meeting biology.

Source: IEEE Spectrum

The strong Mpemba effect, shown in a single qubit

Southeast University researchers used a single photon’s polarization as a qubit and showed that a system starting without quantum coherence can relax to equilibrium exponentially faster, as the theory for the “Davies map” predicted. Published in Physical Review Letters.

Why it’s interesting: Starting farther from equilibrium can mean getting there sooner, and now it is demonstrated in the smallest quantum system.

Source: Phys.org

Trapped ions simulate Aharonov–Bohm interference

A Nature Physics study used two trapped ions on a hybrid qubit–oscillator quantum computer to simulate a Z₂ lattice gauge theory loop: zero flux boosted tunneling through constructive interference, while nonzero flux suppressed it.

Why it’s interesting: Quantum simulators are starting to reproduce gauge-theory physics that classical computers struggle with.

Source: Phys.org

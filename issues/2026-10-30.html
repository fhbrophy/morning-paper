The news
Amazon: AWS grew 39%, but the stock is falling

Amazon's third quarter came in at $201.9 billion in revenue and $1.89 a share, both a little short of estimates. AWS grew 39% to $46.0 billion, its fastest growth in nearly five years, with $17.8 billion in operating income. But free cash flow over the last 12 months swung to a $42.4 billion outflow because of AI spending, and Amazon signaled capex goes up again next year. Shares were down about 6–7% premarket, and Nasdaq 100 futures were down about 2.4%. Analysts at Morgan Stanley, Goldman and others trimmed targets but kept buy ratings.

Why it matters: Your RSUs vest in February, so the stock price matters to you. The other takeaway is that Amazon keeps pouring money into data centers.

Source: Amazon · CNBC · Reuters via Yahoo

Economy: 3.4% growth, inflation still hot

GDP grew at a 3.4% annual pace in the third quarter, up from 2.2%, driven by consumer spending and AI investment. September PCE inflation rose 0.5% for the month and 3.6% from a year ago, pushed by a 9.2% jump in gasoline and energy goods, while core was 3.1%. Jobless claims dipped to 194,000, near the lowest since the late 1960s.

Why it matters: Strong growth plus 3.6% inflation keeps a December rate hike on the table.

Source: BEA · CNBC · AP

Mortgages: Freddie Mac at a one-month low

Freddie Mac's 30-year average fell to 7.24% from 7.32%, and the 15-year to 6.60%. It's the second straight weekly drop of 0.08 points, as the 10-year Treasury eased to about 5.0%. A year ago the 30-year was 6.17%.

Why it matters: Rates are drifting the right way, but they're still about a point above last year.

Source: Freddie Mac · Realtor.com

Data centers: Mesa battery storage, Pima rules

Mesa's Planning and Zoning Board recommended approval of a 42-acre NextEra battery storage project off SR 24 and Loop 202, pitched as support for data center growth in southeast Mesa. Down south, Pima County's planning commission voted 8–1 for rules that would keep data centers in heavy industrial zones, at least a half mile from homes, with noise and water limits. Supervisors vote in December. Glendale's $36 billion Project Eagle is still on track to start construction next month.

Why it matters: Southeast Mesa keeps building power infrastructure for data centers near you, while other parts of the state tighten the rules.

Source: Phoenix Business Journal · KOLD · FOX 10

AI: Mistral opens a giant model, OpenAI rewrites its rules

Mistral released the weights for Mistral Large 4, a trillion-parameter model aimed partly at cybersecurity, a day after Google's security-focused Gemini 4 Carbon. OpenAI also rewrote its Model Spec, the public rules for how its models behave, around five values: following what people actually intend, minimizing harm, honesty, respecting people's own choices, and care.

Why it matters: Big open models mean more companies running AI on their own hardware, which adds data center demand beyond the cloud giants.

Source: TechCrunch · OpenAI · The Neuron

Worth a look
A solar plant that is its own backup

Startup 247Solar is building a small plant in India that focuses sunlight to heat air to about 970°C and stores the heat in golf-ball-sized ceramic pellets, enough to run for 9 to 20 hours after sunset. When the sun falls short, the same turbine can burn fuel instead, which the company says adds less than 10% to the cost.

Why it's interesting: It's a different answer to the overnight backup problem than batteries plus a separate gas plant.

Source: Ars Technica

A writer tried to sneak AI papers past a journal

IEEE Spectrum's writer had free chatbots fake a physics paper, then had an automated research system produce a real one, and sent both to IEEE Access. The fake got caught for made-up references, and the AI-run one got flagged for leaning too heavily on unreviewed preprints, plus a figure with a nonsense label.

Why it's interesting: The filters worked this time, but editors worry they won't keep up.

Source: IEEE Spectrum

Record-fast quantum gates

Researchers at Germany's Jülich research center ran single-qubit operations in 6.8 nanoseconds with better than 99.98% accuracy, by shaping microwave pulses to stop the qubit from jumping to higher energy levels. It's only been shown on one qubit so far.

Why it's interesting: Faster operations leave more time for error correction before qubits lose their state.

Source: Phys.org

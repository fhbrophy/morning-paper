Worth a look
Yeast + Martian sand → printable “living” concrete

Hong Kong engineers mix simulated Martian regolith with gelatin and mussel-protein-coated yeast, then 3D-print cork-size domes that freeze-dry into a porous foam with 10–12 MPa compressive strength — enough for 1–2 stories on Earth, and potentially taller under Mars gravity. Less energy-intensive than melting rock into bricks; yeast can be recycled from dismantled walls.

Why it’s interesting: Materials + bioprocess engineering for extreme environments — adjacent to the “build with what’s on site” mindset of remote critical facilities.

Source: Ars Technica (Chem Circularity, 2026)

GIMLI: robots into a lunar lava-tube skylight

IEEE Spectrum covers the Planetary Science Institute’s GIMLI proposal: a CLPS lander/rover suite with ground-penetrating radar, active-source seismics, and a gravimeter aimed at the Marius Hills Pit to confirm whether a lava tube extends beyond the skylight — a candidate for future lunar habitats.

Why it’s interesting: Subsurface sensing + field robotics for hostile sites; same instrument stack mindset as underground or industrial surveys.

Source: IEEE Spectrum

AI maps when aluminum coatings stop helping

Shibaura Institute researchers used interpretable ML on 90 steam-coated A6061 samples and found that, in higher-roughness films, the roughness–thickness interaction flips near ~2,200 nm film thickness — suggesting roughness shifts from “protective coverage” to “defect pathways.” Model-supported hypothesis, not a finished process recipe.

Why it’s interesting: Clean example of ML that surfaces a physical regime change instead of a black-box score — useful framing for any coatings / corrosion work.

Source: Phys.org (npj Materials Degradation)

Humanoid robots: hype vs. 70% “doesn’t work”

MIT Tech Review’s long look at VLAs, world models, and factory trials argues demos still outrun reliability. Marc Raibert’s line lands hard: jumping from 50% to 70% success “is like it doesn’t work.” Useful home robots may still be ~10 years out; Chinese makers already ship most of today’s humanoid volume.

Why it’s interesting: Separates viral Optimus clips from what’s actually deployable on a factory floor or in a home.

Source: MIT Technology Review

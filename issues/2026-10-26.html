Worth a look
Crows decide twice as fast as we do

In a Science study, two carrion crows judged which way a cloud of dots was moving in about 235 milliseconds, roughly twice as fast as humans and monkeys on the same kind of task, without losing accuracy. Their neurons started encoding the choice about 70 ms after the dots appeared.

Why it's interesting: Bird brains are wired very differently from ours but reach the same answers faster. It's only two birds, though.

Source: Ars Technica · Phys.org

Simulating the universe's first 2 billion years

Harvard-Smithsonian's Lumina is the largest simulation of its kind, a cube about 1.5 billion light-years on a side, run on more than 15,000 GPUs on Oak Ridge's Frontier supercomputer. It's the first to capture helium reionization as it happened, giving astronomers something to check Webb telescope observations against.

Why it's interesting: A good reminder of what all that GPU capacity does besides chatbots.

Source: Phys.org

An AI lab deliberately made its model worse at hacking

Before releasing Kimi K3.1 Pro's weights, Moonshot removed cyberattack material from its training data. The released version solved 81% of in-house exploit tasks, versus 90.5% for one trained with it left in.

Why it's interesting: It shows that open models lagging on hacking ability is partly a choice labs make, not just a gap.

Source: Boring AI

Data centers: Glendale approves Project Eagle, and the grid is tight

Glendale's council approved annexing and rezoning about 160 acres near Litchfield Road and Olive Avenue for three data center buildings, a natural gas plant with 18 turbines and a future APS substation. The city expects about 200 permanent jobs, and construction starts next month. KJZZ reports that APS and SRP both hit summer peaks with only about a 10% reserve margin, below their 15–16% targets. APS has about 4.5 GW promised to data centers, and SRP is building for 10 new data center customers.

Why it matters: More West Valley sites with on-site generation mean more critical facilities jobs nearby, and the power crunch is pushing operators to build their own plants.

Source: ABC15 · KJZZ

AI: OpenAI's cheaper agent model and a bold math claim

OpenAI released GPT-6.1 Terra, a fast model for operating apps on screen, reading technical drawings and everyday writing, at half the API price of its predecessor. Why it matters: AI that can work through ordinary software is getting cheap fast, and safety failures are now showing up in official filings.

Worth a look
A writer tried to sneak an AI-faked paper into an IEEE journal

Benjamin Skuse used free chatbots to write a paper about research that never happened, then used an autonomous research system to produce a real but AI-run study. IEEE Access caught both: the first had a dozen or more made-up references, and the second leaned on too many preprints and had a figure labeled "lhewl."

Why it's interesting: The checks worked this time, but the second paper shows how blurry "real research" is getting.

Source: IEEE Spectrum

Radio waves keep a fusion plasma current running

General Atomics showed the first clear "helicon current drive" in its DIII-D tokamak. A comb-shaped antenna sends megawatt radio waves around the ring, and electrons ride them like surfers, carrying current without the central coil.

Why it's interesting: Today's tokamaks run in pulses of seconds because of that coil. This is one route to running continuously.

Source: Phys.org

Remaking the Roman glass that changes color

The 1,600-year-old Lycurgus Cup looks green in reflected light and red when lit from behind, because of gold and silver nanoparticles in the glass. Sheffield researchers finally worked out a repeatable recipe after testing furnaces, oxygen levels and even sugar, and an artist blew a new sculpture from it.

Why it's interesting: Roman glassmakers were doing nanotechnology by feel.

Source: Ars Technica

Quantum gates hit 6.8 nanoseconds

Researchers report quantum logic gates that run in 6.8 nanoseconds with better than 99.98% accuracy, a speed record at that accuracy level.

Why it's interesting: Faster gates mean more operations get done before the qubits lose their state.

Source: Phys.org

Worth a look
Swift burned up after its robot rescuer failed

NASA’s Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory reentered over Indonesia on November 4 and largely burned up, ending two decades of gamma-ray burst watching. The commercial robot meant to boost it, LINK, spun out of control after losing reaction wheels, burned through its fuel budget, and reentered first in September—never getting close enough to grab the telescope.

Why it’s interesting: Rescuing satellites is its own failure mode: the tow truck can become another piece of debris.

Source: Phys.org / The Conversation

We can’t agree on how to measure emotional control

A large Nature study compared the three usual ways researchers measure “cognitive reappraisal”—questionnaires, task ratings, and amygdala fMRI—and found they barely track each other. Detecting the fMRI effect at 80% power would need about 1,105 people, far more than most studies enroll.

Why it’s interesting: CBT rests on this idea, and Nature published a mostly negative result that says our tools don’t measure the same thing.

Source: Ars Technica

Why big dogs age faster than small ones

ASU researchers using Dog Aging Project data from 864 dogs tied the size–lifespan trade-off to “jumping genes” (LINE1s). Giant breeds lost about 35% more LINE1 methylation each year than small breeds, loosening the brakes that keep those mobile DNA bits from damaging the genome.

Why it’s interesting: Dogs reverse the usual mammal pattern where bigger animals live longer, and this is a clear molecular clue why.

Source: Ars Technica · Science

A single antimatter particle, trapped for months

Northwestern physicists trapped one positron in a near-perfect vacuum of electric and magnetic fields, chilled it to 300 millikelvins, and held it in its quantum ground state for months—the first step toward measuring its magnetic moment as precisely as they’ve done for a single electron.

Why it’s interesting: If the positron and electron moments don’t match, that’s a crack in the Standard Model.

Source: Ars Technica
